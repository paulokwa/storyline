# Product

## Register

product

## What Storyline Is

Storyline is a creative writing workspace for books and screenplays. It combines drafting, structure, characters, ideas, locations, objects, recovery, export, and optional AI help in one calm environment built for sustained writing, not content churn.

It is not an AI toy, a generic note app, or a productivity dashboard with a writing feature added on top. Writing is the product. Organization, recovery, collaboration, and AI exist to support that work.

## Users

### Primary users

- Solo writers drafting a book or screenplay who want a focused place to write and organize story material.
- Early-stage and intermediate writers who want guidance, structure, and reassurance without being forced into a complicated professional workflow.
- Writers who care about ownership, privacy, backups, and having control over where their work lives.

### Secondary users

- Co-writers, editors, beta readers, and collaborators invited into a project for feedback or limited collaboration.
- AI-curious writers who want occasional brainstorming, rewriting help, or scene analysis without making AI the center of their creative process.

### User context

Users are often working alone for long stretches. They may be writing late, returning to a draft after a gap, or trying to regain momentum after losing confidence. They need the app to feel steady, legible, and trustworthy. They should never feel like they are being rushed, gamified, monitored, or sold to while trying to write.

## Core Product Promise

Storyline gives writers a dependable sanctuary for building a story from first spark to structured draft, while keeping the work understandable, recoverable, and under the writer's control.

That promise has five parts:

- Start quickly without learning a complicated system.
- Keep the writing and story structure in one place.
- Preserve work through autosave, recovery, backups, and export.
- Let users choose local-only or cloud-enabled workflows per project.
- Offer AI as optional support, never as the identity of the product.

## Product Philosophy

### Local-first, cloud-optional

Storyline should assume that a writer may want to keep a project private and local to one device. Cloud sync is a choice made for a specific project when the writer wants cross-device access or collaboration. Local mode is not a crippled demo. It is a valid first-class workflow.

Cloud features should be explained plainly:

- what cloud sync changes
- what stays local
- what remains under the writer's control
- what collaboration features become available

The app should never imply that every serious user must move to the cloud.

### AI is optional writing support

AI is a tool inside the workspace, not the product story. Storyline should remain useful, coherent, and welcoming when AI is disabled, unavailable, or intentionally ignored.

AI should help with:

- brainstorming
- scene analysis
- tone exploration
- rewriting assistance
- idea expansion

AI should not dominate the visual hierarchy, the navigation, the onboarding, or the emotional tone of the product. The app should still feel like a writing environment first.

### Trust before cleverness

Writers are putting fragile work into the product. The interface should favor clarity over novelty, reassurance over hype, and stable behavior over fashionable complexity.

## Brand Personality

Calm, warm, trustworthy.

Storyline should feel like:

- a quiet creative workspace
- a paper-and-pencil craft made digital
- a tool that respects attention
- a patient guide rather than an eager assistant

It should not feel clinical, over-optimized, startup-loud, or theatrically futuristic.

## Tone Principles

- Speak plainly. Users should understand what a feature does on first read.
- Sound writer-focused, not platform-focused.
- Prefer reassuring specificity over abstract claims.
- Avoid inflated language about creativity, genius, transformation, or disruption.
- Avoid corporate policy phrasing when simple human language will do.
- In high-stakes moments, sound calm and concrete.
- When explaining AI, keep the tone practical, bounded, and honest.

## Trust and Privacy Principles

- The user's story belongs to the user.
- Storage mode must always be understandable: local-only, cloud-enabled, and collaboration states must be clearly distinguished.
- Privacy-sensitive behavior must be explained before it happens, not hidden in fine print.
- AI use must never feel sneaky. Users should understand when content is being sent for AI assistance and what context is included.
- Backup, recovery, export, and file operations should feel dependable and respectful of user control.
- Collaboration permissions must be easy to understand, especially for read-only versus editing access.
- Destructive actions should be explicit, calm, and hard to trigger by accident.

## Anti-references

Storyline should not look or sound like:

- a generic AI copilot product
- a corporate SaaS dashboard
- a crypto, growth-hack, or hype-driven startup brand
- a dark neon command center
- a social feed for writers

Avoid language and visuals that suggest:

- constant optimization
- algorithmic authority
- surveillance
- hustle culture
- magical AI omniscience

## Design Principles

- Writing comes first. The draft, the structure, and the immediate next action must stay clearer than secondary features.
- Calm confidence beats spectacle. The product should feel intentional and composed, never loud.
- Local and cloud are choices, not status levels. Both paths should feel legitimate and supported.
- AI stays in a supporting role. It may be useful, but it should never overpower the writer's own work.
- Trust is built through clarity. Storage, permissions, recovery, export, and privacy behavior should always be legible.

## Accessibility and Inclusion

- Prioritize clear hierarchy, legible type, and strong enough contrast for long reading sessions.
- Avoid relying on tiny helper text, faint gray labels, or color alone to communicate state.
- Support keyboard-first use where practical, especially in writing and navigation flows.
- Motion should remain subtle and non-essential.
- Interfaces should remain understandable to first-time writers, not just power users.
